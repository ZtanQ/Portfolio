The first run of the waste classification model for Smart Kitchen returned an F1 of 1.0 on the test set. Instead of celebrating, I stopped. Classifiers do not achieve perfect scores on real problems. When they do, they are almost always seeing something they shouldn't.

## What was happening

The target variable was whether an inventory movement ended as waste. Somewhere in the pipeline I was computing a derived feature — days from purchase to consumption or disposal — using the same disposal date that defined the label. The model wasn't predicting waste. It was reading the answer.

## How to catch it

Three practical signals:

1. The metric is too good for the problem. For waste classification with imbalanced classes, a macro F1 above 0.85 already deserves a second look.
2. Feature importance concentrates on a single variable, and that variable happens to involve the same information as the label.
3. The model collapses when you remove that feature. This is the cleanest test.

## What I did

I rebuilt the temporal split and reconstructed the feature using only information available at purchase time. The real macro F1 landed near 0.72 — a far more believable number, and one I can defend to anyone reading the report carefully.

The lesson isn't "don't trust metrics." It's that a model that looks too good is explained more often by a leaking pipeline than by a brilliant architecture.
